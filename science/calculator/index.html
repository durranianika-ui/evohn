At the bench

Dilution calculator 

Concentration is quantity divided by volume, and a great many preparation errors come from treating one of those as fixed. This converts between all three and shows the arithmetic, so the figure that ends up in a preparation record is one you have checked rather than one you have been given.

Inputs
Quantity · Volume · Target
Outputs
Concentration · Draw volume
Basis
Assayed content
Scope
Arithmetic only
mg

Use the assayed content from the certificate rather than the label strength where the two differ.

mL

The total volume of bacteriostatic or sterile water introduced. Record this — concentration is meaningless without it.

mg

Optional. The amount of compound a single withdrawal is intended to contain.

Concentration

2.50 mg/mL

5 mg ÷ 2 mL. This is the figure every subsequent calculation depends on.

Volume to withdraw

0.100 mL

0.25 mg ÷ 2.50 mg/mL.

On a U-100 graduation

10.0 units

A U-100 syringe is graduated in hundredths of a millilitre; this is that reading, not a dose.

The working

  • Concentration (mg/mL) = quantity (mg) ÷ diluent volume (mL)
  • Volume to draw (mL) = target quantity (mg) ÷ concentration
  • U-100 graduation = volume (mL) × 100

Doubling the diluent halves the concentration. The quantity in the vial has not changed — which is where most preparation errors begin.

Worked reference

Common preparations

The same vial at different dilutions. Nothing about the quantity in the vial changes across these rows — only the volume it is dissolved into, and therefore the concentration.

Two figures that shift the answer

  • Assayed content. The certificate states what the laboratory found, which is rarely identical to the label. For a compound consumed stoichiometrically, that difference goes straight into the result.
  • Water content. Karl Fischer titration reports how much of the weighed mass is water rather than compound. The effect stacks with any content variance.
